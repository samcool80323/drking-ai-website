End-of-year health fund extras: a recall campaign that fits

For most funds, unused extras benefits reset on 1 January. Here is how to run a dental check-up recall that is timely, respectful and consent-safe.

Key takeaways

  • Most large funds reset extras limits on 1 January, but some funds reset on 1 July — never assume every patient shares the same clock.
  • Unused extras generally do not roll over on calendar-year covers, which makes late November and December the natural recall window.
  • Write fund-neutral messages: point patients to their fund app for balances, and never name a benefit amount.
  • Campaign texts need consent, sender identification and an opt-out honoured within five working days.
  • Measure replies, bookings and attended visits, and tag the campaign source so next year is easier to improve.

Any dental check-up recall campaign lands differently in late November. For most members of large Australian health funds, unused extras benefits reset on 1 January — Bupa publishes that its yearly limits run over the calendar year, and Medibank says the same for most extras services — so a patient with an untouched check-up benefit is usually most open to a reminder in the last weeks of the year. But not every fund runs on that clock: the published statement for one fund, Defence Health, shows benefits resetting on 1 July instead. A campaign that fits is one that gives patients the right information, never claims to know their exact fund rules, and stays inside consent law. This guide covers what the reset dates actually are, who to include, wording that respects patients, and what to measure.

When do extras limits actually reset?

There is no single Australian reset date, and that is the first thing to accept before you build anything. Bupa describes its yearly limit as the maximum you can claim for a service over a calendar year, 1 January to 31 December, and states that anything unclaimed does not roll over — it resets on 1 January. Medibank puts it plainly too: for most extras services, annual limits reset on 1 January, and unused limits do not carry over. Other funds publish different dates. The Standard Information Statement for a Defence Health extras cover, published on the government-run privatehealth.gov.au comparison site, states that benefits reset on 1 July each year. Same benefit pool, different finish line.

How do you find your own patients' reset dates without calling every fund? You mostly do not need to. The campaign works because it is fund-neutral: it tells the patient what usually happens to unused extras, points them to the fund app or member statement, and asks nothing. If your front desk wants a quick reference, keep a private cheat sheet of the reset patterns published by the funds most of your patients belong to — but keep it for staff planning only. The moment a specific date appears in a patient message, you are guessing on their behalf.

Treat that table as a planning starting point, not a message script. Published wording changes as products change, and one fund can run different dates for different services, so check a fund's current pages before you lean on any row above. The safest sentence in the whole campaign is also the simplest: check your fund app for what you have left.

There is a staffing angle too. A recall wave that lands in early December produces booking enquiries at exactly the time your front desk is juggling holiday rosters and reduced hours, so plan who answers replies and when. If your December hours change, say so in the message — patients who book a nine in the morning slot for a practice that closed the week before will not thank you, and the missed visit then needs its own follow-up. Small touches like stating reply hours, or promising an answer by the next morning, keep the campaign from feeling like a machine on the other end.

What the reset means for a dental chair

An unused check-up benefit is an appointment a patient has effectively already paid for through premiums. When the year turns on a calendar-year cover, that value disappears, because nothing rolls over. Practices see the demand side of this every year: December diaries fill with check-up and clean requests while early January starts quiet. The practices that benefit are the ones whose recall lists are already clean — patients who are due, patients who said yes to treatment and never booked, and patients whose last visit is quietly stretching past twelve months.

See the loop below. The reset creates the moment, the message gives the nudge, and the booking turns it into chair time. The only parts you control are the message and the diary space you deliberately keep for it — a recall wave sent into a diary with no December openings just moves frustration from the patient to your front desk.

It works in the other direction too. Patients who were treated mid-year and need a follow-up in December — a crown seated, a review booked and dropped — belong on the same list as recalls, because their appointment also has a natural deadline: the year ends, the benefit resets, and the unfinished work quietly ages. Our guide to following up unscheduled treatment covers that second group in detail.

  1. Late NovemberClean the recall list: remove opt-outs, duplicate numbers and anyone seen recently.
  2. Early DecemberSend wave one to due patients who have consent, with a booking link.
  3. Mid DecemberOne follow-up to non-responders only. Stop there.
  4. Final weekReply to every enquiry fast; December diaries close early.
  5. 1 JanuaryMeasure replies, bookings and attended visits; action every opt-out.

Who to include, and who to leave out

Not every patient should get this message. A reset-based recall works when it reaches someone with a real reason to book: a check-up that is due, a treatment plan that was accepted and never scheduled, or a visit history that is drifting past a year. Start from what your practice software already knows — last visit date, treatment plans accepted without a booking, and reminders already sent this year. If your list is built from a hunch instead of those three filters, you will send half the campaign to patients who were never going to book and annoy the rest.

Do

  • Include patients due for a check-up, or with unscheduled treatment
  • Point to the fund app for balances — check what you have left
  • Include a working opt-out on every message
  • Keep real diary space open before you send

Avoid

  • Naming a benefit amount or promising what a fund will pay
  • Sending to anyone who opted out, at any time
  • Stacking email, SMS and calls into one week
  • Attaching an inducement without checking advertising rules first

Two of those need spelling out. First, benefit amounts: you cannot see a patient's remaining balance, and benefit tables differ by fund, policy level and service, so any dollar figure in your message is a guess — describe the appointment, not the money. Second, offers: a plain reminder that a check-up is due is routine recall communication, but the moment something free or discounted is attached, advertising rules for regulated health services start to matter and the wording needs care. This is general information, not legal advice; when in doubt, keep the message simple and the offer out of it.

List hygiene deserves its own hour before anything sends. Fix the obvious: duplicate numbers from merged records, mobiles entered with missing digits, and family members sharing one number. Decide who on the team handles a patient who replies with something unrelated — a billing question, a referral query — because a good campaign can still collapse into a bad experience if the reply lands with someone who cannot help. Write those answers down before the first message goes out, not after the first awkward reply arrives.

Message templates that fit

The templates below are fund-neutral on purpose. They tell the patient what usually happens to unused extras, promise nothing, and leave the decision with the patient. Adapt the placeholders, keep the opt-out, and send from a number that can receive replies — a reply that lands nowhere is worse than no reply, because the patient tried.

Example text thread

Hi [First name], [Practice] here. If you have check-up benefits left, most funds reset them on 1 January. Our December diary fills early — book here: [booking link]. Reply STOP to opt out.

9:41 AM

How much of my limit is left?

10:02 AM

Your fund app shows your remaining limits — we cannot see your balance. Would a Saturday morning suit?

10:07 AM

One practical detail: if you send from a branded SMS header (an alphanumeric sender ID, like your practice name), ACMA notes those headers generally cannot receive return messages — so a reply STOP instruction may go nowhere. Send from a reply-capable number, or give a phone number or web page as the opt-out route instead. And keep a note of who replies STOP: the request must be actioned within five working days, which in a December campaign means during the busiest week your front desk has.

Example follow-up thread

[Practice]: Hi [First name], just checking you saw our note — your check-up is due before the year ends. Tue 17 or Sat 21 Dec? Reply STOP to opt out.

11:05 AM

Sat 21, morning if possible

11:20 AM

We have 9:20 am that Saturday. Reply 1 to hold it and we will confirm.

11:26 AM

Email template

Subject: Your check-up before the year turns

Hi [First name], a quick note before the holidays. Many large health funds reset extras limits on 1 January, though some reset later in the year — your fund app or member statement will show yours. If your cover is on a calendar year, an unused dental check-up benefit usually does not carry over. If you are due for a visit, we would love to see you: [booking link]. If now is not the right time, no problem — we will be here in January. — The [Practice] team

Not everything needs to be a text. Patients who have never replied to an SMS in years may respond better to a two-line voicemail or a call from a familiar name, and some older patients prefer the phone outright. The same rules of thumb apply in every channel: fund-neutral wording, no benefit amounts, one clear action, and a real person reachable if the patient wants to talk. Whatever mix you choose, keep the channel list short — two touches in one channel beat four touches across three.

Consent and opt-out, briefly

Campaign texts and emails are commercial electronic messages under the Spam Act 2003, which ACMA administers. Before you send: have consent for each recipient, identify your practice as the sender, and make opting out easy — ACMA requires unsubscribe requests to be honoured within five working days, without fees or logins. For express consent, ACMA recommends keeping a record of who consented, when and how. This is general information, not legal advice.

A five-step campaign plan

Keep the campaign small enough to run alongside everything else a December front desk is doing. Five steps, one list, one channel, one follow-up. If the first wave reaches the right patients with the right wording, more waves are unnecessary; if the wording is wrong, more waves only multiply the damage and the opt-outs.

  1. Pull the listDue recalls, unscheduled treatment plans, consent confirmed, opt-outs removed
  2. Draft and checkFund-neutral wording, booking link, opt-out, one clear action
  3. Send wave oneEarly December, from a number that can receive replies
  4. Follow up onceSeven days later, non-responders only, then stop
  5. Measure and cleanLog replies, bookings, attendances and opt-outs the same day

This December push is one spoke of a wider recall system. What to measure, and what good looks like

Decide what success means before the first message goes out, or the campaign turns into a feeling rather than a result. Bookings matter more than replies, and attended visits matter more than bookings — a patient who rebooks for February after a December cancellation did not become an active patient this year. Set targets as a practice and compare campaigns against your own history, rather than against industry benchmarks nobody can verify for you.

  • Replies received, and how fast the front desk answered each one
  • Bookings made from the campaign, tagged to its source
  • Attended visits, not just bookings made
  • Opt-outs, actioned within five working days
  • Patients still unbooked after the follow-up wave
  • December chair gaps compared with your last few years

Two of these connect to work you are probably already doing. Appointment reminders close the gap between a booked visit and an attended one, so the attended-visit number is where reminders and recalls meet. And source tracking tells you which touchpoint — the recall text, the follow-up, or a web search that found your booking page — actually produced the booking, so you can invest in the right channel next year. Questions practice managers ask

Good questions.
Clear answers.

Short answers to the questions that come up most.

Do all health funds reset extras limits on 1 January?

No. Bupa and Medibank publish that their yearly or annual extras limits reset on 1 January, while the published statement for a Defence Health extras cover shows benefits resetting on 1 July each year. Some covers also run on policy anniversary dates. Because the dates differ by fund and sometimes by service, the patient's own fund app or member statement is the only reliable source for their situation.

Can I text patients about their unused extras?

Yes, if you stay inside the consent rules. Campaign texts are commercial electronic messages under the Spam Act 2003, so you need consent for each recipient, your practice identified as the sender, and an easy opt-out honoured within five working days. ACMA also notes that alphanumeric sender IDs generally cannot receive replies, so use a reply-capable number or give an alternative opt-out route.

Can we tell a patient how much they have left to claim?

No — practices cannot see a patient's health fund balance, so any amount in a message would be a guess. Benefit limits differ by fund, policy level and service, and some services share a combined limit. Frame the message around the due check-up and the booking link, and let the patient check their remaining limits in the fund's own app or member statement.

How many follow-up messages should the campaign include?

Keep it to two: the first message in early December, and one follow-up to non-responders about a week later. More waves mostly generate opt-outs, and every extra message needs consent and a working opt-out of its own. If a patient does not reply after the second message, leave them to your regular reminder cycle rather than sending again this year.
