Inactive dental patients: recall vs lapsed

A practical way to separate due-for-recall patients from long-gap records, check permission and build a dental follow-up list that the front desk can actually use.

Key takeaways

  • Inactive is an administrative flag, not a clinical diagnosis; the dentist sets individual review timing.
  • Six, twelve, eighteen and twenty-four months are example reporting bands, not universal recall intervals.
  • Exclude existing bookings and opt-outs, separate unfinished treatment and check permission before sending.
  • Measure eligible people, replies, bookings, attended visits and opt-outs separately.

“Inactive” sounds like a simple database status. At a dental practice, it can hide very different stories: someone due for a routine review, someone with an unfinished treatment plan, someone who has moved, and someone who asked not to hear from you. One bulk message cannot serve them all. Before you run a recall campaign, decide what the label actually means.

What counts as an inactive dental patient?

An inactive dental patient is someone whose last completed visit falls outside a practice-defined follow-up window and who has no upcoming appointment. There is no universal six-, twelve- or twenty-four-month rule that fits every person or treatment. Your dentist sets clinically appropriate review timing; your practice manager defines an administrative flag so the team can find records needing review. The flag is not a diagnosis or a statement that a patient has neglected care.

Start with three separate questions: when was the last completed visit, is another booking already in the diary, and what follow-up did the clinician actually recommend? A due-for-recall patient and a person who accepted treatment but never booked need different conversations. Someone who declined further contact should not be pulled back into a marketing list simply because their last visit was long ago.

  1. Find the last visitUse completed visits, not cancelled slots.
  2. Check the next bookingExclude people already scheduled.
  3. Read the care contextSeparate recall from unfinished treatment.
  4. Check contact permissionRespect opt-outs and channel preferences.

How should a practice use six, twelve, eighteen and twenty-four months?

Treat these intervals as example reporting bands, not prescribed dental recall intervals. They help a manager ask better questions about the database. A person last seen six months ago might already have a clinician-set review due; another might not. The source of truth for clinical review timing is the patient’s care plan, not a generic calendar. The Australian Dental Association emphasises patient-centred care and discussion of treatment options; the practice should retain that individual approach rather than turning a marketing segment into clinical advice in its partnering policy.

  1. 6 months since visitExample early review band: check the recorded due date before contacting.
  2. 12 months since visitExample overdue band: distinguish a missed recall from a future booking.
  3. 18 months since visitExample lapsed band: verify contact details and the reason for follow-up.
  4. 24 months since visitExample long-gap band: review expectations and consent before any campaign.

Why make several bands? A practice might discover that its “inactive” dashboard includes people who moved away, people who came in last month under a duplicate record, and people awaiting a planned visit at another location. If you collapse these into one count, the campaign looks bigger but becomes less relevant. Begin with a small, manually reviewed sample of each band. Ask the front desk what each record really represents; record the exception types and adjust the filter before sending anything.

The bands also let you measure your own process without claiming that a particular interval is clinically right. For each band, count eligible patients only after excluding opt-outs, deceased patients, duplicates, booked patients and those whose clinician notes say not to contact. Do not export diagnostic details into a marketing spreadsheet just to make the list easier to sort. A person needs only the minimum information required for a respectful administrative message.

Recall or reactivation: which conversation is this?

A recall follows an individual care or review plan, such as a reminder that a previously discussed check-up may be due. Reactivation is a practice-led attempt to reconnect with someone who has not visited for a while. The words can overlap in a dashboard, but the purpose and consent analysis may differ. The OAIC’s guidance on communications with patients explains why expected care-related contact is not automatically the same as a broad message promoting services to a whole database.

Put the patient’s reason for contact first. A dental check-up reminder should not quietly turn into a promotion for cosmetic treatment. If the message offers or promotes a service, check the rules that apply to marketing, even if the sender calls it a “recall”. For SMS and email marketing, the ACMA says to have consent, identify the sender and provide a working way to unsubscribe. Record the original permission and how it can be withdrawn. A previous visit alone is not a blank cheque for any future campaign.

Do

  • Use the clinician’s recorded review plan.
  • Check whether a booking already exists.
  • Separate care follow-up from promotion.
  • Offer a simple way to reply or opt out.

Avoid

  • Treating every old record as campaign-ready.
  • Inferring a diagnosis from a date alone.
  • Calling a sales offer a care reminder.
  • Repeating a message after an opt-out.

What fields belong in a useful segment?

A useful export does not have to be a giant clinical data dump. For an administrative review, start with a patient identifier held securely within the practice system, last completed visit, upcoming booking status, recorded recall due date if present, preferred contact channel, and a consent or opt-out status. Add a staff review flag for uncertain cases. Keep sensitive treatment details with the treating team; a staff member can decide whether the proposed wording fits the care context before a message is sent.

Ask who owns the exception list. If a person has an unscheduled treatment plan, the dentist or designated staff member should decide whether and how to follow up. Our separate guide to unscheduled treatment follow-up covers that conversation; do not mix it with a generic “time for a check-up” blast. If an appointment was missed and never rebooked, the no-show follow-up guide is a better starting point. A precise segment prevents three teams from contacting the same person for three different reasons.

  • Last completed visit is reliable, with duplicate records resolved.
  • No future booking already covers the need.
  • Clinician-set review timing is checked where relevant.
  • Unfinished treatment is routed to an appropriate human reviewer.
  • Channel permission and any opt-out are visible before sending.
  • The practice has a clear owner for replies and booking requests.

Review the exclusions with the people who will deal with the replies. If the team cannot tell why a patient was selected, it will struggle to answer “why did you message me?” The purpose should be simple enough to explain in one sentence. Keep the list small enough to follow through on every reply. A campaign that generates conversations nobody owns is not a useful recovery plan.

What does a respectful first message sound like?

Lead with the practice identity and a choice, not a warning. Ask whether the person would like to arrange a visit or speak with the team. Avoid putting treatment names, diagnosis, unpaid amounts or anxiety labels in a text that could appear on a shared screen. If you are sending a marketing SMS, include a functional opt-out such as “Reply STOP” only if the sender can actually receive and process replies. The ACMA notes that some alphanumeric sender IDs cannot receive return messages. Supply your normal contact details in the final message and test the unsubscribe path before the send.

Example text thread — adapt only for eligible contacts

Hi [First name], it’s [Practice]. If you’d like to arrange a visit or talk to our team, reply here or call [practice phone]. Reply STOP to opt out.

Example outgoing SMS

Thanks. Could someone call me about available times?

Example reply

Do not tell someone they are “overdue” or “inactive” without checking what was agreed at the last visit. People may have changed practices, deferred care because of cost or transport, or simply prefer not to be contacted. A warm reply can be useful even when the answer is “not now”: record the preference and stop there. The practice should not offer clinical reassurance or make a recommendation from an automated thread; a clinician decides clinical questions.

A patient is not a database status; the message should make room for their choice.

Where do privacy and advertising checks fit?

Run them before the send, not after a patient complains. The OAIC’s health privacy guidance distinguishes the original purpose of collecting health information from other uses. Its patient communications guidance asks providers to consider the context and whether consent can reasonably be implied for a particular message. For broad promotional messaging, do not assume a historical relationship automatically covers a new purpose. Keep a usable record of the permission your practice relies on.

Check the copy as well as the list. Ahpra’s summary of advertising requirements says health-service advertising must be accurate and not misleading, and must not use testimonials about clinical aspects of care. There is no need to promise a result, apply artificial urgency or show a patient story to offer someone a way to contact the practice. These are real-world operating rules, not a search-ranking technique. Have your practice’s responsible person review any campaign that blends care reminders with promotion; this article is general information, not legal or clinical advice.

How do you know the list is useful?

Define the denominator first: eligible people in the segment after exclusions, not every old record. Then record messages attempted, delivered where that information exists, replies, requests to book, bookings made, visits attended and opt-outs. Label each clearly. A reply is not a booking, and a booking is not an attended visit. Your own figures will show where the process needs attention; do not borrow a generic conversion benchmark to declare the campaign a success or failure.

In a weekly review, sample the replies that required manual handling. Was the patient already booked? Did they ask a care question the front desk could not answer? Did a contact preference fail to carry across systems? Log the correction in the source record and update the filter. Keep a separate tally for people who asked not to be contacted; that is a boundary to respect, not a “lead” to win back.

  1. Count eligibilityRemove duplicates, bookings and opt-outs.
  2. Send a small batchMake sure the team can handle replies.
  3. Record outcomesSeparate replies, bookings and visits.
  4. Improve the ruleReview exceptions before expanding.

This turns a one-off campaign into a repeatable routine. The patient reactivation calendar shows how to plan contact over time; the reactivation overview covers the wider workflow. Those resources are useful only when the underlying records and permissions are trustworthy. Short answers to the questions that come up most.

When is a dental patient considered inactive?

A practice can define an administrative inactivity flag using the last completed visit and the absence of a future booking. There is no single time interval that is appropriate for every patient. Check any clinician-set recall plan and the reason for contact before deciding whether the person belongs in a care reminder, a reactivation campaign or neither.

Is a six-month dental recall right for everyone?

No. A six-month band can be useful for database reporting, but it is not a universal clinical schedule. The treating dentist decides review timing based on the individual patient and the recorded care plan. Before sending a reminder, confirm the due date and whether an appointment is already booked; do not derive clinical advice from a spreadsheet interval.

Can a practice text someone who has not visited for two years?

A long gap alone does not establish permission to send a marketing SMS. Review what the patient agreed to, any opt-out, the purpose of the message and whether contact details remain suitable. For marketing texts, the ACMA requires consent, sender identification and a working unsubscribe method. A care-related reminder needs its own contextual assessment; seek appropriate advice for uncertain records.

What should the front desk do with an unscheduled treatment plan?

Keep it separate from a generic overdue-recall list. A designated member of the team should review the recorded context and ask the clinician where a clinical decision is needed. Offer a way for the patient to discuss next steps, without assuming why they paused or promising an outcome. Record a declined contact preference and do not keep sending automated messages.
