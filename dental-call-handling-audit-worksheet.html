The dental call-handling audit that shows where calls leak

Most dental practices count the patients in the chair, not the calls that never became one. Here is a 14-day audit that shows exactly where the phone leaks.

Key takeaways

  • Vendor data suggests roughly three in ten dental callers hang up before anyone answers — only your own fortnight of logs shows your rate.
  • A call can leak four ways: not answered, after hours, dropped mid-call, or answered but never booked.
  • Log date, time, who answered, caller type, reason and outcome for two weeks; five numbers tell the story.
  • After-hours and lunchtime gaps are structural, not front-desk failures — fix them with process, not blame.
  • Give each leak one owner and one fix, then re-run the audit to see whether the number moved.
A dental call-handling audit is a two-week log of each inbound phone call: when it arrived, who picked up, what the caller wanted, and whether it ended in a booking. Run it for a fortnight and the guesswork about missed calls stops — you can point to the hour they cluster in and the reason each one was lost. This guide covers what the published data says about dental phone calls, the four places a call leaks, and a 14-day method any practice can run with a shared spreadsheet. It pairs with our longer missed-call audit guide for dental teams.

What the published data says about dental phone calls

First, the context. The phone is still how patients arrange care: a 2025 survey of American adults published in Health Affairs Scholar found phone calls were the most common way to schedule a medical appointment, used by 72.1% of respondents — ahead of booking in person (40.6%) or through an online portal (34.1%).

What happens to those calls is less reassuring. Patient Prism, a call-analytics vendor, scored 11.55 million patient calls to 8,280 dental practices and DSO locations on its platform during 2025. For every 100 of those calls, 69 reached the front desk, 31 hung up before reaching an agent, 34 were genuine booking opportunities, and 21 ended in a booked appointment. Among the 13 callers per 100 who walked away without booking, fewer than 5 received a follow-up call, and fewer than 1 came back and booked.

A February 2026 analysis by Peerlogic of one 26-location dental group — 4,280 calls tracked in a single month — reported a 62% average answer rate, meaning 38% of callers got no response. Among answered calls, conversion averaged 40%: new patient calls converted at 25.24%, while existing patient calls converted at 55.77%. The single most common reason a caller did not book was the call disconnecting before the booking was made.

Read all of this as shape rather than benchmark. Both call datasets come from vendors whose customers self-select, the survey is American, and Patient Prism does not define its hang-up category, which may include after-hours calls, callers on hold and voicemail. We could not verify any peer-reviewed Australian study that sets a dental answer-rate benchmark. That is exactly why the rest of this article is about counting your own calls for two weeks.

The four places a dental call leaks

Lost calls are not one event. When a practice says it missed a few calls, four different failures hide inside that sentence, and each has a different fix. The funnel above shows where they sit.

1. Rang out or hit voicemail during opening hours. The visible failure, and the only one most practices count. A caller with a broken tooth at 12:40 pm while the desk is at lunch will not keep trying — they will call the next practice on their list.

2. Arrived outside opening hours. Nobody was rostered, so the call was lost by design rather than by neglect. These calls need their own count, because the fix — a voicemail that points to your online booking page, missed-call text-back, or reviewed opening hours — is structural rather than behavioural.

3. Answered, then dropped. Hold queues, transfers and mid-call disconnects count as answered in the phone report and lost in the diary. In the Peerlogic dataset above, disconnects were the most common reason callers did not book.

4. Answered, but not booked. New patients hesitate: fees, availability, parking, what the first visit involves. The call ends with a promise to think about it, and nobody owns the follow-up. The 30-point conversion gap between new and existing patients in the Peerlogic data lives here.

Your audit has one job: work out which of the four is biggest for you, because the fixes do not overlap.

After hours is a structural gap, not a staff failure

Patients ring when life allows: after the school run, after work, on the drive home. If the practice is closed, those calls fail before anyone picks up, and nothing that happens inside opening hours will change it. Treat after-hours as a design question rather than a staffing one.

During the audit, log after-hours calls separately from in-hours misses. Most phone systems keep an unanswered-call or voicemail history you can transcribe once a day. Note the hours they cluster in — practices are often surprised how early the evening calls start and how many arrive before the doors open.

Not a staff failure

If most of your leaks are structural — lunch coverage and after hours — the fix belongs to the roster and the phone setup, not to the people at the desk. Say this out loud when you present the audit, or the exercise turns into blame and the next audit gets fudged.

The 14-day audit, step by step

Fourteen days is long enough to show a weekly rhythm and short enough that the team sustains it. Pick two ordinary weeks: avoid public holidays, school holidays if your patient mix shifts, and weeks with staff on leave. The goal is a fair picture of a normal fortnight, not a record one.

  1. Before day 1Create the log (a shared spreadsheet is fine), agree what counts as answered, and brief the team at the morning huddle.
  2. Days 1 to 7Log each inbound call as it happens, or at the end of each hour on busy days. Change nothing else about how the phone is handled.
  3. Day 8Tally week one in ten minutes. Early leaks usually show already — lunch hours and mid-morning are common.
  4. Days 8 to 14Keep logging. Resist fixing anything mid-audit; changes make the two weeks impossible to compare.
  5. Day 15Tally the five numbers below, name the biggest leak, and give it one owner and one fix.

What to record for each call

Six fields per call keeps the log fast enough to survive a busy Tuesday:

  • Date, time and day of week — leaks cluster around lunch and mid-morning
  • Who answered — a person, voicemail, or nobody
  • New or existing patient — they behave differently on the phone
  • What the caller wanted — a new appointment, a rebook, an enquiry, accounts
  • Outcome — booked, not booked, call dropped, message taken
  • Follow-up owed and who owns it — the column most practices skip

Five numbers worth counting

At the end, five numbers tell the story. If your analytics or phone system can compute any of them, use it; otherwise count by hand — five numbers from a fortnight of logs is minutes of work.

What good looks like, practice-defined

There is no published Australian benchmark for dental answer rates that we could verify, and the vendor figures quoted above are ranges from self-selected customers. So the standard that matters is your own: the fortnight you just measured is your baseline, and the target you set from it is practice-defined.

A workable pattern: pick one number, set one target, give it a date. If the answer rate across the fortnight was 71%, an 80% target with a review date one month after your first fix is a real target. Five aspirational numbers with no owner is not.

Your most useful benchmark is your own fortnight of call logs, not a vendor average.

From log to fixes: a simple loop

An audit that ends in a spreadsheet is decoration. Close the loop with a short sequence, one leak at a time:

  1. Tally the five numbersFifteen minutes, one page
  2. Name the biggest leakNot the loudest — the biggest
  3. Give it one ownerA name, not a team
  4. Make one changeLunch cover, voicemail routing, or a text-back message
  5. Re-run in a monthCompare like with like

Some fixes are free and take an afternoon: a lunch-cover arrangement, a clearer voicemail. Some are tools, which is where the next section comes in.

Messages that recover missed calls

The cheapest recovery for leak number one is a text. The caller hung up, but the phone system knows the number, and a short message within minutes turns a lost call into a conversation. This is the missed-call text-back pattern — our missed-call recovery guide covers the mechanics. It works best when a reply lands with someone who can actually help.

Example missed-call text-back thread

Hi [First name], this is [Practice]. We missed your call earlier — sorry about that. Reply here and we will help or find you a time. Reply STOP to opt out.

Keep the message short, name the practice, and make stopping easy.

Consent and opt-outs

ACMA guidance on the Spam Act 2003 asks three things of commercial electronic messages: consent, sender identification, and a working way to opt out. A reply to a missed call is usually a service message rather than marketing, but the habit is the same: say who you are, and make it easy to stop — the wording above includes an opt-out line for that reason.

Short answers to the questions that come up most.

How long should a dental call-handling audit run?

Two weeks is the practical minimum. One week is not enough because call patterns differ between Monday mornings and Friday afternoons, and a fortnight smooths out one-off spikes. Pick ordinary weeks: a week with a public holiday, a team member on leave or a big marketing push will describe that week, not your practice.

Do we need special software to run the audit?

No. A shared spreadsheet with six columns and the phone system call history covers the essentials. Most phone systems can also show unanswered calls and after-hours activity, which saves transcription time. Call-tracking tools help at scale or across multiple locations, but a disciplined fortnight of manual logging answers the question a single-site practice actually has.

What counts as a missed call?

Define it before day one and write the definition into the log. A workable one: an inbound call during opening hours that no team member answered within a time you set. Calls that arrived outside opening hours get their own count, and calls that dropped after being answered get a third, because each points to a different fix.

Is there a benchmark for how many calls dental practices miss?

Not one with a published method that we could verify. The figures in this article come from call-analytics vendors whose customers self-select, and from one American survey. Treat vendor ranges as a sign of scale, not a standard. Your own two-week baseline is the benchmark that matters, and improving on it is the goal.
