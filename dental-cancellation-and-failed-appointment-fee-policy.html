Key takeaways

  • Tell patients the notice window, possible fee and change route before confirming a booking.
  • Review the actual circumstances before charging a missed-appointment fee; leave room for exceptions.
  • Keep appointment reminders distinct from marketing and use a working contact and opt-out route.
  • Use one consistent policy across online booking, phone scripts and messages.

A dental cancellation policy should make it easy for a patient to tell the practice early when they cannot attend, explain what happens after a late cancellation or missed visit, and leave room for individual circumstances. A fee is a choice for the practice, not the purpose of the policy. Set your own notice period and fee only after checking your actual costs, booking terms and applicable consumer rules.

What should the policy say first?

Start with the action you want: “Please contact us as soon as you know you cannot attend.” Then tell patients how to contact you, what notice window your practice uses, whether you charge a fee, how much it is, when you may waive it and how someone can query a charge. Show the policy before the booking is confirmed, not for the first time after a missed visit. The practice can also remind patients of the terms at confirmation and in a pre-appointment message.

A failed appointment is a booking that did not go ahead because the patient did not attend or cancelled too late for the practice to use the reserved time. It is an administrative description, not a judgement about the patient. Illness, caring duties, transport, financial pressure and sudden events can make even a well-intentioned person miss a visit. The front desk needs a simple route to review exceptions without having to argue about them in an automated text.

  1. Tell people earlyDisplay terms before confirmation.
  2. Make contact easyOffer a clear number or reply route.
  3. Review the contextDo not auto-charge on a flag alone.
  4. Offer another timeKeep the care conversation open.

There is no magic notice window or dollar amount that suits all dental appointments. A short check-up and a longer reserved procedure may need different operational arrangements. Decide how your practice communicates these, what booking evidence it keeps, and who has authority to waive or dispute a charge. The ACCC explains contracts and unfair terms; a posted notice is not a substitute for checking whether a term is fair and properly disclosed.

A plain-English policy you can adapt

The wording below is an editing template, not a legal rule or a fee recommendation. Replace every square-bracket item with your real practice settings. If you do not charge a fee, say so instead of leaving the fee paragraph in place. Have the final wording checked for the services you provide and the state or territory where you operate.

Patient-facing policy template

“If you need to change or cancel your appointment at [Practice], please contact us on [phone] as soon as you can. We ask for [practice-selected notice period] notice where possible, so we can offer the time to someone else. If you cancel with less notice or do not attend, a fee of [practice-approved amount or clear calculation] may apply where this was explained when you booked. We consider unexpected illness, emergencies and other individual circumstances. Please call [phone] if you would like to discuss a change, a fee or a new appointment.”

Use wording patients can understand without interpreting internal codes. “May apply” gives staff room to review exceptions, but it must not hide the actual fee or how the decision is made. State the amount or a clear method of calculation at the point of booking. If you have different terms for different appointment types, make those differences visible and confirm which set applies to a particular booking.

Keep the policy accessible at the point of online booking and with the reception team. A patient who books over the phone should hear the material terms before accepting the appointment. Record the version of the policy provided and the time the booking was made. If you later change the policy, do not assume old bookings silently inherit the new terms. Review that scenario before applying a revised fee.

Do

  • Show the amount and notice window before booking.
  • Give a real contact route and exception process.
  • Use the same wording across phone, web and reminders.

Avoid

  • Surprising patients with a fee they never saw.
  • Calling every non-attendance deliberate.
  • Using a punitive blanket fee without review.

For a useful check, ask a person outside your team to read the policy and answer three questions: “How do I cancel?”, “What might I pay?”, and “Who can I speak to if something unexpected happened?” If any answer is missing, simplify the document. This review is about clear communication; it does not certify a particular policy as legally enforceable.

When should a failed-appointment fee be reviewed?

Before a fee is issued, confirm the booking details, the terms the patient received, the contact history and the reason recorded for the missed slot. Was the appointment actually confirmed? Did the practice change its time? Did the patient try to reach the team through an advertised channel? Was there a health or access barrier that needs an individual response? These are practical review questions, not automatic legal tests.

If your system marks someone “DNA”, treat it as a prompt for a human review. The person answering the phone should not have to make a clinical judgement, and a chatbot should not pronounce on someone’s circumstances. Escalate a disagreement to the person authorised to decide. Keep any reason given in the appropriate private record, not in a general marketing list or a group message.

  • Confirm the correct patient, appointment, time and booking status.
  • Check which policy and fee were communicated before confirmation.
  • Look for cancellation attempts across the channels you offered.
  • Ask about unexpected circumstances without demanding sensitive details over SMS.
  • Record who approved, waived or disputed a charge and why.
  • Offer a clear route to discuss rebooking and the underlying care plan.

The ACCC describes consumer guarantees for services; you cannot write away those rights in a policy. Fees and terms depend on the booking and local circumstances. This guide offers general operational wording, not legal advice. Obtain suitable advice before adopting or enforcing a charge, especially for a disputed or complex booking.

What should reminder and follow-up messages sound like?

A routine appointment reminder is not a marketing blast. ACMA distinguishes non-promotional appointment reminders from commercial messages. Keep reminders focused on the booked appointment, the change route and the policy link if appropriate. Do not tack on a promotion: that can change the character of a message. Check patient contact preferences and privacy expectations before sending.

Example appointment reminder

[Practice]: Your appointment is [day] at [time]. Need to change it? Call [phone] or reply here. Policy: [link]. Reply STOP to opt out of texts.

Practice message

I can't make it. Could I speak to reception?

Example reply

That first example assumes a reply-capable SMS channel. If a sender name cannot receive replies, provide a working phone or unsubscribe route instead of telling patients to reply STOP. Separate a request to stop marketing messages from any safety-critical or care-related contact preferences. Do not rely on a template to decide what communications are appropriate for a particular patient.

For a missed visit, lead with care and the practical next step rather than an accusation. Give the team space to check the record before mentioning a fee. If the patient replies with a reason, respond personally; do not automatically feed the explanation into a campaign. The companion guide on following up after a no-show focuses on keeping a patient connected rather than treating a missed appointment as a lost person.

Example missed-visit follow-up

[Practice]: We missed you today. If you'd like another appointment or need to talk about your booking, call [phone] or reply here. Reply STOP to opt out of texts.

Practice message

Could I call tomorrow? Something came up.

Example reply

If you do send a promotional dental campaign, it needs its own consent and unsubscribe checks. ACMA says commercial messages require consent, sender identification, contact details and a functional unsubscribe method. A message advertising a service is not magically an appointment reminder because it mentions a booking. Keep that difference explicit in the staff workflow and in your sending tool.

Where should patients encounter the terms?

Think of the patient's actual journey, not merely a policy page hidden in a footer. At online booking, put the material terms close enough to the confirmation action that a person can read them. On the phone, have a short plain-language script and note that the team explained it. In a reminder, give the change route without turning the text into a dense contract. When a patient cancels, acknowledge it and confirm whether any fee is under review.

  1. Before bookingShow the policy and explain any fee and notice window.
  2. At confirmationConfirm the appointment and how to contact the team.
  3. Before the visitSend a clear reminder through an appropriate channel.
  4. After a changeConfirm cancellation, review the context and offer another time.

The order matters. If the first mention of the charge comes after a missed appointment, the team starts the conversation on the back foot. If the first reminder has no working change route, a patient may try to cancel and fail. A clear contact path gives reception a chance to release the slot and arrange a new visit. For the practical communication side, see DrKing's dental no-show page.

When the practice itself must change an appointment, contact the patient promptly and offer a way to choose another time. A fair policy should describe what the team will do, not just what patients must do. Review accessibility, language needs and channel choices. Someone unable to use a web portal still needs a straightforward phone route.

A good cancellation policy helps patients tell you early—not fear telling you at all.

How can a dental practice make the policy consistent?

Give one team member ownership of the wording and a second person responsibility for checking how it appears in booking forms, confirmation messages and phone scripts. Train front-desk staff on when to escalate. A scripted fee demand is a poor substitute for a clear review route. Keep a version date so the team knows which terms were in force for a particular booking.

A short weekly review can reveal friction without promising a particular result. Look at which appointment types generate late changes, whether people had a usable cancellation channel, and whether any disputed fees came from inconsistent wording. Count cancelled in time, cancelled late, did not attend, rebooked and fee waived as separate administrative categories. Set the thresholds yourself; none is an industry benchmark supplied by this article.

Track the categories to improve communication, not to label patients as unreliable. Share trends with the practice owner, not individual stories in marketing copy.

Short answers to the questions that come up most.

Can an Australian dental practice charge a no-show fee?

A practice may consider a missed-appointment fee, but a fee should not be presented as automatic or universally enforceable. Check the booking terms that the patient actually received, the circumstances of the missed visit and applicable consumer rules. Show the amount or a clear method of calculation before confirmation, provide a review route and obtain advice before enforcing a disputed charge.

How much notice should a patient give to cancel a dental appointment?

There is no universal notice period in this template. A practice should select and clearly communicate a window appropriate to its bookings and operations, then review the actual circumstances when a patient cannot meet it. Explain how to make contact, including for patients who cannot use an online booking portal. A longer reserved appointment may need a different operational process from a short visit.

Should a missed-appointment text mention a fee?

Usually the first follow-up works better as a respectful check-in with a route to discuss or rebook the visit. Before referring to a fee, confirm which terms were disclosed, whether the patient tried to cancel and who has authority to review an exception. Keep clinical details out of an SMS. If the text becomes promotional, separately check consent, sender details and unsubscribe requirements.

Can appointment reminders be sent by SMS without marketing consent?

ACMA says messages that are only appointment reminders generally are not commercial spam, but adding an offer or promotion may change that. Check the patient’s contact preferences and the privacy context as well. If you send marketing messages, ACMA requires consent, sender identification and a working unsubscribe facility. Use a reply-capable channel for a “Reply STOP” instruction or give another functioning route.
